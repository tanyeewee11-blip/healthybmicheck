Healthy BMI for Women — What You Need to Know

Last updated: May 2025  ·  7 min read

BMI is calculated the same way for men and women, but interpreting the result requires understanding some important differences in female physiology. Women naturally carry more body fat than men at the same BMI, and factors like age, hormonal changes, and reproductive history all influence what a healthy BMI looks like at different life stages.

What Is a Healthy BMI for Women?

According to the World Health Organization, the standard healthy BMI range for adult women — as for men — is 18.5 to 24.9. However, research suggests that the health implications of BMI values shift across a woman's lifespan, particularly around key hormonal transitions like the menopause.

Summary: For most adult women, a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is associated with the lowest health risks. For women over 60, a BMI between 22 and 27 may be more appropriate, as lower values in older women often reflect muscle loss rather than a lean, healthy physique.

Why Women Have Higher Body Fat at the Same BMI

Women naturally have a higher percentage of body fat than men at the same height and weight — and therefore the same BMI. This is not a health problem; it is a biological requirement. Women's bodies store essential fat in areas including the breasts, hips, pelvis, and thighs to support reproductive function and hormonal health.

As a result, a healthy body fat percentage for women (21–33%) is significantly higher than for men (8–24%), even when both have the same BMI. This also means that body fat percentage is sometimes a more informative health indicator for women than BMI alone.

How Hormonal Changes Affect BMI and Body Composition

During reproductive years (18–45)

Weight can fluctuate by 1–3 kg throughout the menstrual cycle due to water retention and hormonal changes. This is normal and not a reflection of actual fat gain or loss. It is worth being aware of this when interpreting BMI or weight measurements taken at different points in the cycle.

During pregnancy

BMI is not an appropriate health measure during pregnancy. Weight gain during pregnancy is normal and necessary. The amount of healthy weight gain recommended during pregnancy depends on pre-pregnancy BMI and should be discussed with a midwife or doctor.

During perimenopause and menopause (40–55)

The hormonal changes of menopause — particularly declining oestrogen — lead to a shift in fat distribution from the hips and thighs to the abdominal area. This change in body composition can increase cardiometabolic risk even without a significant change in total weight or BMI. Waist circumference becomes a particularly important indicator during this period.

Many women notice weight gain during perimenopause even without changes in diet or activity. This is largely driven by hormonal changes affecting metabolism and appetite regulation, not simply "slowing down."

Post-menopause (55 and over)

After menopause, muscle mass tends to decline more rapidly, which can cause BMI to remain stable while body fat percentage increases. Strength training becomes especially important during this phase to preserve lean mass and maintain metabolic health.

Waist Circumference — An Important Companion to BMI

Research consistently shows that where fat is stored is at least as important as how much fat a person carries. Abdominal (visceral) fat — stored around the waist and internal organs — carries higher health risks than fat stored in the hips and thighs.

For women, a waist circumference above 80cm (31.5 inches) indicates increased health risk, and above 88cm (34.6 inches) indicates substantially increased risk, regardless of BMI. Measuring your waist alongside calculating your BMI gives a more complete picture of your health.

What If Your BMI Is Outside the Healthy Range?

If your BMI is below 18.5 (underweight)

A BMI below 18.5 in women can be associated with hormonal disruption, bone density loss, anaemia, and reduced immune function. If you are underweight, particularly if you have irregular or absent periods, it is important to speak with a healthcare professional. Underweight BMI in women is sometimes associated with disordered eating patterns that benefit from professional support.

If your BMI is 25–29.9 (overweight)

A BMI in this range increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, and some cancers over time, though individual risk varies significantly based on fitness level, diet quality, and fat distribution. Many women with a BMI in this range are metabolically healthy, particularly if they are physically active.

If your BMI is 30 or above (obese)

A BMI of 30 or above is associated with meaningful increases in health risks. Even a modest weight loss of 5–10% of body weight can produce significant improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels.

Important: BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. A single BMI reading does not define your health. Many other factors — physical fitness, diet quality, blood markers, stress, and sleep — all contribute to overall health. Always consult a healthcare professional for a personalised assessment.

Beyond BMI — Other Indicators for Women's Health

  • Waist circumference — below 80cm is generally low risk for women
  • Body fat percentage — healthy range for women is approximately 21–33%
  • Resting heart rate — a lower resting heart rate generally indicates better cardiovascular fitness
  • Blood pressure — ideally below 120/80 mmHg
  • Blood glucose and cholesterol — key metabolic markers worth monitoring annually after 40
