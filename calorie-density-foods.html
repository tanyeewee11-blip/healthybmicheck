Low Calorie Density Foods That Keep You Full

Updated 2026 06  ·  Based on peer-reviewed research  ·  8 min read

One of the most powerful but least discussed weight loss concepts is calorie density: the number of calories per gram of food. Foods with low calorie density let you eat large, satisfying portions while consuming fewer total calories. This is the science behind why people on plant-based or Mediterranean diets often lose weight without feeling deprived.

The principle: Your stomach has stretch receptors that trigger fullness based on volume — not calories. Filling your stomach with low-calorie-density foods activates these receptors and triggers satiety hormones, producing fullness at a fraction of the calories of high-density foods.

Calorie Density Categories

Categorykcal per 100gExamples
Very low (eat freely)0–60 kcalVegetables, broth, most fruit, plain yogurt
Low (eat liberally)60–150 kcalLegumes, lean meat, fish, eggs, whole grains
Medium (portion aware)150–300 kcalBread, pasta, rice, starchy vegetables
High (eat sparingly)300–500 kcalCheese, chocolate, fried foods, pastries
Very high (small amounts)500–900 kcalOils, butter, nuts, seeds, biscuits, chips

Best Low-Calorie-Density Foods

Non-starchy vegetables (10–50 kcal/100g)

  • Cucumber: 15 kcal/100g
  • Lettuce/spinach: 15–20 kcal/100g
  • Broccoli: 34 kcal/100g
  • Courgette/zucchini: 17 kcal/100g
  • Bell peppers: 31 kcal/100g
  • Tomatoes: 18 kcal/100g
  • Mushrooms: 22 kcal/100g

Fruit (45–80 kcal/100g)

  • Strawberries: 32 kcal/100g
  • Watermelon: 30 kcal/100g
  • Apple: 52 kcal/100g
  • Orange: 47 kcal/100g

Lean protein (100–165 kcal/100g cooked)

  • Chicken breast (cooked): 165 kcal/100g
  • White fish (cod, tilapia): 90–100 kcal/100g
  • Shrimp/prawns: 85 kcal/100g
  • Egg whites: 52 kcal/100g

Soups and broths

Broth-based soups are among the most filling foods per calorie. A 400ml bowl of vegetable soup contains 50–100 kcal but occupies significant stomach volume. Studies show eating soup before a meal reduces total calorie intake at that meal by 20–25%.

Practical Strategies

Start meals with a salad or soup

Large portions of low-calorie-density food at the beginning of a meal fill stomach volume and reduce total intake from the more calorie-dense main course. This single habit can reduce daily calorie intake by 200–400 kcal without conscious restriction.

Swap high-density for low-density versions

  • Rice → Cauliflower rice (saves ~200 kcal per cup)
  • Pasta → Courgette noodles (saves ~150 kcal per serving)
  • Potato chips → Air-popped popcorn (saves ~400 kcal per 100g)
  • Juice → Whole fruit (saves ~100 kcal and adds fibre)

⚠️ Calorie density is a useful framework but not a rule to follow rigidly. Nuts, seeds, and avocados are calorie-dense but nutritionally valuable. Context and overall diet quality matter more than the density of any single food.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to count calories if I focus on calorie density?

Not necessarily. Many people who focus on eating predominantly low-calorie-density foods lose weight without tracking calories, because they naturally eat fewer calories while feeling full. This approach works best for people who find calorie counting stressful. However, if weight loss stalls, tracking calories for 2–4 weeks can identify hidden high-density foods that are preventing progress.

Are all vegetables low in calories?

Most non-starchy vegetables are very low in calories (15–50 kcal/100g). However, starchy vegetables — potatoes, sweet potatoes, corn, peas — are significantly higher (70–110 kcal/100g cooked) and should be portioned more carefully during a calorie deficit. They're still nutritious and filling, but not as freely consumed as leafy greens or cucumbers.

Why are nuts high-calorie if they're healthy?

Nuts are calorie-dense (500–700 kcal/100g) because of their high fat content — but the fat is predominantly healthy unsaturated fat with significant nutritional benefits. The key is portion control: a 30g serving of almonds (~170 kcal) provides excellent nutrition. Eating nuts directly from a large bag makes it easy to consume 400–600 kcal without noticing.

Does cooking affect calorie density?

Yes. Cooking vegetables in oil increases their calorie density significantly — a tablespoon of oil adds 120 kcal to whatever it's cooked with. Steaming, boiling, air-frying, or eating raw preserves the low-calorie-density benefit. The cooking method is often more calorie-impactful than the vegetable itself.
