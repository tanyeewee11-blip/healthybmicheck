How Much Water Should You Drink Per Day?

Last updated: May 2025  ·  6 min read

Water is essential for nearly every function in the human body — from regulating temperature and transporting nutrients to supporting kidney function and cognitive performance. Yet most people have little idea whether they are drinking enough, too little, or occasionally too much.

This guide explains how to calculate your personal daily water needs and how factors like body weight, activity, and climate affect your requirements.

The "8 Glasses a Day" Rule — Is It Accurate?

The popular recommendation of eight 250ml glasses (2 litres) per day is a convenient guideline but not a universal truth. It originated from a 1945 US dietary recommendation that was later taken out of context. The actual amount varies significantly based on individual factors.

A more reliable approach is to base your intake on your body weight, activity level, and the climate you live in.

Simple baseline: A common evidence-based starting point is approximately 35ml of water per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 70kg adult, that is 2.45 litres — close to the 8-glasses guideline, but adjusted for your actual body size.

How Body Weight Affects Water Needs

Larger bodies require more water to maintain cellular function, regulate temperature, and support organ health. This is why a 50kg person and a 100kg person have very different hydration requirements, even if they live in the same climate and have the same activity level.

How Climate Affects Hydration — Especially in the Tropics

People living in hot, humid climates like Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, and Thailand have significantly higher water needs than those in cooler climates. Sweating is the body's primary cooling mechanism, and in a tropical climate, you can lose 0.5–1 litre of fluid per hour during moderate outdoor activity.

For people in tropical climates, adding 0.5–1 litre to your base daily intake is a reasonable adjustment, particularly if you spend time outdoors or in non-air-conditioned environments.

How Exercise Increases Water Requirements

Physical activity dramatically increases fluid loss through sweat and respiration. A moderately intense 60-minute workout can result in 0.5–2 litres of sweat loss, depending on body size, intensity, and environmental temperature.

A practical rule of thumb: drink an additional 500ml of water for every 30–45 minutes of moderate exercise. For intense or prolonged sessions, consider a drink with electrolytes to replace sodium and potassium lost through sweat.

How to Tell If You Are Drinking Enough

The simplest and most reliable indicator of hydration is the colour of your urine.

Other signs of dehydration include dry mouth, headache, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and reduced urine frequency. Many people misinterpret mild dehydration as hunger, which can lead to unnecessary snacking.

Practical Tips to Drink More Water

  • Start every morning with a glass of water before coffee or tea — it rehydrates you after 7–8 hours without fluids.
  • Carry a reusable water bottle and set a target based on your bottle size (e.g. finish three 700ml bottles throughout the day).
  • Set hourly reminders on your phone until drinking regularly becomes a habit.
  • Eat water-rich foods — cucumber, watermelon, strawberries, and leafy greens all contribute meaningfully to daily fluid intake.
  • Drink a glass of water before each meal — this also slightly reduces calorie intake at mealtimes.
  • Flavour your water with sliced lemon, cucumber, or mint if plain water feels unappealing.

Does Coffee and Tea Count?

Yes — to a meaningful degree. Despite caffeine's mild diuretic effect, research consistently shows that moderate consumption of coffee and tea still results in a net positive contribution to daily fluid intake. You do not need to subtract your morning coffee from your water target.

Alcohol, however, is a genuine diuretic and leads to increased fluid loss. For every alcoholic drink, consuming an additional glass of water helps offset the dehydrating effect.

Can You Drink Too Much Water?

In healthy adults with normal kidney function, it is very difficult to drink too much water under normal circumstances. The kidneys can process roughly 0.8–1 litre per hour. Hyponatraemia (dangerously low blood sodium from excess water) is almost exclusively seen in endurance athletes who drink large amounts without adequate electrolyte replacement over several hours.

For everyday purposes, drinking to thirst and monitoring urine colour is a perfectly safe and effective strategy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the '8 glasses a day' rule have scientific backing?

No — the '8 glasses a day' guideline has no specific scientific basis and is not supported by current research or any major health authority. Individual water needs vary significantly based on body size, activity level, climate, and diet. Using urine colour (pale straw yellow = well hydrated) is a more reliable indicator than a fixed number of glasses.

Does drinking water help you lose weight?

Yes, through several mechanisms: drinking 400–500ml before meals reduces calorie intake at those meals in clinical studies; replacing sugary drinks with water reduces calorie intake significantly; and mild dehydration is sometimes confused with hunger, leading to unnecessary eating. Water doesn't directly burn fat, but supports a calorie deficit.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes — hyponatraemia (low blood sodium from excessive water intake) is rare but real, occurring primarily in endurance athletes who drink very large volumes of plain water during prolonged exercise. For most healthy adults, the kidneys can process 0.8–1.0 litres per hour. Drinking to thirst, plus slightly more in hot or exercise conditions, is safe for the vast majority.

Does coffee or tea count toward daily water intake?

Yes — caffeinated beverages like coffee and tea contribute meaningfully to daily fluid intake. Despite caffeine's mild diuretic effect, research shows that regular coffee and tea consumers retain the majority of fluid from these drinks. Black coffee, tea, and herbal infusions all count toward hydration. Sugary drinks also count for hydration but add significant calories.
